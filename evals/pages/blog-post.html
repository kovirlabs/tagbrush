What I learned running a tiny SaaS for a year

Twelve months, 214 paying customers, and one very boring tech stack.

By Maya Lindqvist · October 28, 2026 · 9 min read

A year ago I quit my job to build Invoicely, a small tool that turns time-tracking exports into clean invoices for freelancers. It now pays my rent. It does not yet pay for a second person. Here is what I would tell myself on day one.

1. Distribution beats features

For the first four months I shipped a feature a week and grew by roughly three customers a month. In month five I stopped building and wrote six honest guides on freelance invoicing. Signups tripled. Nobody asked for the features I had been shipping; they asked for the guides.

The lesson is not "content marketing works." It is that I had no idea where my customers already spent their time, and I should have found out before writing code.

The product you build is a guess. The people you talk to are data.

A note I taped above my desk in month three

2. Boring infrastructure is a feature

Invoicely runs on one server, one Postgres database, and a cron job. My total hosting bill is $41 a month. I have had two outages, both caused by me.

The single most useful piece of code I wrote all year is the nightly job that checks for failed payments and emails the customer before Stripe gives up:

// jobs/dunning.js: runs nightly at 02:00
import { db, stripe, mail } from "../lib/index.js";

export async function remindFailedPayments() {
  const failed = await db.query(
    `SELECT id, email, stripe_invoice_id
       FROM accounts
      WHERE payment_status = 'past_due'
        AND last_reminder_at < now() - interval '3 days'`
  );

  for (const account of failed.rows) {
    const invoice = await stripe.invoices.retrieve(account.stripe_invoice_id);
    await mail.send(account.email, "payment-failed", {
      amount: invoice.amount_due / 100,
      payUrl: invoice.hosted_invoice_url,
    });
    await db.query(
      "UPDATE accounts SET last_reminder_at = now() WHERE id = $1",
      [account.id]
    );
  }
}

That job alone recovered about 9% of revenue that would otherwise have churned silently.

4. Support is the product

I answer every support email myself, usually within two hours. Customers mention this more than any feature. A surprising number of upgrades start with a reply that says "thanks for fixing that so fast."

I keep a plain text file of every question I have been asked more than twice. When a line hits five, it becomes either a help article or a fix in the product.

5. Protect the boring days

The hardest part was not technical. It was the long stretches where nothing happened: no signups, no feedback, no outages. I learned to schedule those days: Mondays for writing, Tuesdays and Wednesdays for code, Thursdays for talking to customers, and Fridays off unless something is on fire.

Year two goal: reach $8,000 MRR and hire a part-time support person, so I can take a real vacation without checking Cmd + R on the Stripe dashboard.
